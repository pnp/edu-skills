# Create Rubric Student Explainer

Turn an educator's rubric into a concise set of student-facing slides. Preserve the rubric's criteria, performance levels, scoring meaning, and expectations while making the distinctions easier for students to understand and use before submitting work.

## Student-Facing Voice

Write all visible explainer content directly to the student. Use clear second-person phrasing built around `You should...`, `Your work should...`, and `Before you submit, check that you...`.

- Translate criterion descriptors into direct expectations, such as `You should explain how the selected radiation is produced, used, and affects matter.`
- Use `Your work should...` when describing observable qualities of the submission.
- Use `To move to the next level, you should...` when explaining differences between adjacent performance levels.
- Keep sentences concise, concrete, age-appropriate, and focused on actions the student can take.
- Avoid teacher-facing narration such as `The student demonstrates...`, `The response includes...`, or `Students are expected to...` in visible slide content.
- Do not address students with deficit labels such as `You are poor at...`. Describe what their work currently shows and what they should add, correct, clarify, or support.
- Keep rubric-fidelity notes, scoring caveats, ambiguities, and educator decisions in speaker notes rather than the student's main reading path.

## Gather the Source

**A source rubric is required, and the source rubric must never be overwritten.**

1. Use the selected rubric, current slide, attached document or image, speaker notes, or educator-provided text as the authoritative source.
2. Prefer a Microsoft 365 Teach rubric when one is selected, attached, pasted, or referenced. Read the full rubric before drafting the explainer.
3. When available, also read the related assignment directions, grade or learner level, subject, learning objective, expected product, and any supplied examples.
4. If no rubric is available, stop and ask the educator to provide or select it. Do not invent assessment criteria or performance descriptors from only a topic.
5. Do not modify or overwrite the source rubric. Create explainer slides in the current presentation or in a clearly labeled companion presentation, as requested.

## Parse a Teach Rubric

Teach rubrics commonly use a matrix with:

- An assignment or rubric title
- Criterion names in rows
- Ordered performance levels in columns
- One descriptor at each criterion-and-level intersection
- Optional points, weights, or scores

Map the matrix exactly before simplifying it. For the supplied example, the structure includes criteria such as `Scientific Accuracy`, `Evidence-Based Benefits and Risks`, `Radiation Type Distinction`, `Key Vocabulary`, and `Clarity of Communication`, with levels `Excellent`, `Good`, `Fair`, and `Poor`.

Do not assume every Teach rubric uses those names or four levels. Copy every custom performance-level label exactly as written, and preserve the source criterion order, level order, points, weights, and any blank cells. If a title, score, weight, or descriptor is missing or unreadable, mark it `[Needs educator review]` rather than guessing.

## Preserve Rubric Fidelity

Create a traceable mapping from every source descriptor to its student-facing explanation.

1. Keep each criterion's meaning and assessed construct unchanged.
2. Keep performance levels in their original order and preserve any scores or weights exactly.
3. Retain essential disciplinary terms. Define them in student-friendly language rather than replacing them with imprecise wording.
4. Preserve qualifiers that distinguish levels, including accuracy, completeness, independence, evidence quality, frequency, clarity, and degree of error.
5. Do not collapse two levels into one, strengthen or weaken a descriptor, add a new requirement, or imply that an omitted requirement is scored.
6. Do not turn subjective descriptors into precise counts unless the source rubric gives those counts.
7. Put the original descriptor text in speaker notes for its criterion slide when the visible slide uses a shorter paraphrase.
8. Flag contradictions, duplicated descriptors, overlapping levels, missing distinctions, or ambiguous terms for educator review. Do not silently repair the grading rubric.

## Plan the Explainer

Use the smallest useful slide set. Default to:

1. **Rubric overview:** assignment or rubric title, purpose, performance-level order, and how students should use the explainer.
2. **One slide per criterion:** criterion meaning, top-level look-fors, distinctions among levels, and an optional grounded example.
3. **Student checklist:** concise self-check statements derived from the highest performance descriptors across all criteria.

For a short rubric with simple descriptors, combine two related criteria on one slide only when text remains readable and each criterion stays distinct. For a large rubric, use section dividers or continuation slides rather than shrinking text.

## Build the Rubric Overview

1. Use the source rubric or assignment title. Correct an obvious truncation only when the intended title is confirmed; otherwise retain it and flag it.
2. State that the explainer helps students understand the rubric and does not replace the educator's original scoring rubric.
3. Show all performance levels in source order with their exact labels and scores when supplied.
4. Explain how to use the deck in direct student language, such as `You should review each criterion, compare the quality levels, and check your work before you submit.`
5. Do not present the lowest level as a label for a student. Describe characteristics of the work, never the learner.

## Build Each Criterion Slide

For every rubric criterion:

1. Use the exact criterion name as the slide title or visible subtitle.
2. Add a short `What you should do` explanation appropriate to the stated learner age.
3. Add `Your work should` look-fors derived only from the highest performance descriptor. Write them as observable features students can check in their own work.
4. Explain the performance progression in source order. Use `To move to the next level, you should...` to clarify differences between adjacent levels.
5. Preserve uncertainty or vague wording from the source and flag it in teacher notes rather than inventing a sharper scoring rule.
6. When useful, add one example, non-example, annotated excerpt, worked step, or visual model that demonstrates the criterion.
7. Use an educator-supplied student example when available. Remove names and identifying details before placing student work in the deck.
8. If no example is supplied, create only a brief illustrative example grounded in the assignment, rubric, and source content. Label it `Illustrative example`, and do not imply that it is the only acceptable response.
9. Do not create an example when doing so would reveal an answer, complete the assessed reasoning for the student, or misrepresent a creative or open-ended task.

## Explain Quality Levels

- Use the rubric's exact level names, even when they differ from common labels such as `Excellent`, `Proficient`, `Developing`, or `Beginning`.
- Describe expectations directly: `You should include...` or `Your work should show...`, never `You are poor at...`.
- Make the top level concrete without presenting perfection as the only successful outcome.
- Clarify what is missing, partial, inaccurate, unsupported, or unclear at lower levels using the source descriptor's meaning.
- Do not shame, rank, diagnose, or make predictions about students.
- Do not use color alone to signal quality. Pair color with the exact level label and source score when available.

## Create the Student Checklist

Derive one concise checklist item from each criterion's highest performance descriptor.

1. Begin each item with `You should` and an observable action, such as `You should explain`, `You should support`, `You should identify`, `You should use`, or `You should organize`.
2. Preserve all required parts of the top descriptor without adding new scoring requirements.
3. Keep the criterion name visible beside or above each item so students can connect the checklist to the original rubric.
4. Use editable checkboxes and leave enough space for printed or digital self-review.
5. Add a final prompt such as `Which criterion will you improve before submitting?` only when reflection fits the assignment.
6. Do not add requirements that are absent from the highest descriptors.
7. State that the checklist supports self-review and does not calculate or guarantee a score.

## Build the Slides

1. Preserve the current Slide Master, assigned layouts, theme colors, theme fonts, margins, branding, and slide size.
2. Use native editable text, shapes, tables, and images. Do not flatten the explainer into screenshots.
3. Give each slide one main purpose and use consistent placement for criterion name, meaning, look-fors, level comparison, and example.
4. Avoid reproducing the full dense rubric matrix on every slide. Include the original matrix once when readable, or link/refer to the educator's source rubric.
5. Use restrained visual cues to distinguish levels and criteria. Avoid decorative gradients, 3D effects, unrelated stock imagery, and icons that imply judgment about students.
6. Put original descriptors, traceability notes, source details, ambiguities, and facilitation guidance in speaker notes.
7. Do not add animations unless requested. If progressive reveal is requested, use simple Appear or short Fade effects and keep all content accessible in print or PDF.

## Sensitive Information and Fairness

- Remove student names, IDs, grades, comments, and other personal information from examples unless the educator explicitly requests inclusion and has appropriate permission.
- Do not infer accommodations, disability, language proficiency, or ability from the rubric or student work.
- Use inclusive examples and avoid stereotypes.
- Do not change the rubric to make it easier or harder for a perceived learner group. Explain the educator's rubric as written and flag accessibility barriers separately.

## Accessibility

- Keep text readable from the intended viewing distance. Split dense criteria across slides rather than reducing text excessively.
- Use sufficient contrast and do not rely on color, position, icons, or animation alone to communicate performance level.
- Add descriptive alt text to meaningful examples, diagrams, and controls.
- Maintain a logical reading order: title, criterion meaning, look-fors, performance levels, example, then self-check prompt.
- Use clear table headers when a table is retained and keep each descriptor associated with the correct criterion and level.
- Keep essential content understandable in edit view, Slide Show, printed handouts, and PDF export.

## Validation

Review the complete explainer against the source rubric at fit-to-window and in Slide Show mode.

1. Confirm every source criterion appears once and in the original order unless an intentional regrouping is reported.
2. Confirm every performance level uses the exact source label, order, score, and weight when supplied.
3. Compare every student-facing explanation with its source descriptor and confirm no requirement was added, removed, strengthened, or weakened.
4. Confirm visible explanations, level comparisons, and checklist items consistently use direct second-person language such as `You should`, `Your work should`, or `To move to the next level, you should`.
5. Confirm top-level look-fors and checklist items cover every required part of the highest descriptor without promising a score.
6. Confirm adjacent performance levels remain meaningfully distinct and no ambiguous source language was silently rewritten into a new grading rule.
7. Confirm examples are source-grounded, de-identified, labeled when illustrative, and do not reveal answers or constrain valid responses.
8. Confirm the explainer says it supports understanding and self-review but does not replace the original rubric or calculate a grade.
9. Confirm no text, table, image, label, or control is clipped, overlapped, distorted, or too small.
10. Confirm contrast, alt text, reading order, table headers, and print/PDF comprehension.
11. Report the source used, rubric title, criterion count, level count, slides created, examples added, checklist mode, assumptions, ambiguities, and educator decisions still needed.
