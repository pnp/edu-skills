# Create Vocabulary and Concept Builder

Create a short, student-ready PowerPoint sequence that helps learners understand, connect, and use essential vocabulary and concepts. Build more than a glossary: show what each term means, what it looks like in context, how it differs from nearby ideas, and how students can retrieve and apply it.

## Gather the Instructional Context

1. Identify the subject, grade or learner level, lesson topic, learning objective, available time, source material, and number of terms or concepts requested.
2. Use selected slides, speaker notes, educator-provided files, or the educator's prompt as the factual basis.
3. Prefer a Microsoft 365 Teach lesson plan when one is selected, attached, or referenced. Read its title, `Overview`, `Standards`, `Objectives`, `Materials`, and timed `Lesson Outline` before selecting vocabulary.
4. When using a Teach lesson plan, prioritize terms that students need to understand the stated Objectives or complete the Lesson Outline. Preserve the lesson's terminology and intended meaning.
5. Ask for missing information only when the topic, learner level, or source meaning cannot be inferred. Use the defaults below for optional choices and report them.

**Source accuracy:** do not invent definitions, facts, quotations, examples, etymologies, translations, standards, symbols, formulas, or source claims. Flag conflicting, ambiguous, or unsupported source language for educator review.

## Select the Terms and Concepts

Choose the smallest useful set for the available time.

- Default to four to six essential terms or concepts for one lesson.
- Prioritize words and ideas that are central to the objective, recur in the source, carry discipline-specific meaning, or are prerequisites for later reasoning.
- Include academic language only when students must use it to understand directions, discuss evidence, or produce the expected response.
- Exclude words that are already familiar at the stated learner level unless they have a specialized meaning in this lesson.
- Treat symbols, formulas, processes, models, and categories as concepts when understanding them requires more than a verbal definition.
- Distinguish closely related terms rather than presenting them as interchangeable.
- Preserve required technical vocabulary. Make it understandable without replacing it with an inaccurate simpler term.

If the source contains more than eight essential terms, group them into coherent sets and create separate sections or decks. Do not crowd a single slide with a long word list.

## Default Learning Sequence

Unless the educator specifies another format, create:

1. **Vocabulary and concept map:** title, learning objective, essential question when supplied, and the selected terms arranged by a meaningful relationship.
2. **One focused slide per term or concept:** disciplinary definition, student-friendly explanation, visual representation, example, non-example or boundary case, and one brief use prompt.
3. **Connect the concepts:** compare, classify, sequence, cause-and-effect, part-to-whole, or another source-supported relationship task.
4. **Retrieval check:** a short mixed practice slide that asks students to recall and apply the terms without simply copying the definitions.
5. **Optional response reveal:** concise answers or discussion guidance hidden behind a trigger or placed on a linked answer slide.

For younger learners or a brief pre-teach, use fewer terms and more oral or visual practice. For secondary, higher education, or technical subjects, preserve precise disciplinary distinctions and require context-appropriate use.

## Build Each Term or Concept

Use the elements that teach the idea; do not force every element when it would be redundant or misleading.

1. **Term:** use the exact source term, symbol, phrase, or concept name.
2. **Disciplinary definition:** preserve the accurate subject-specific meaning. Cite or identify the supplied source in speaker notes.
3. **Student-friendly explanation:** restate the meaning in concise, age-appropriate language without weakening important conditions or distinctions.
4. **Meaningful visual:** use a labeled diagram, process, model, image, number line, timeline, map, or other representation that helps explain the concept.
5. **Example:** show a clear instance grounded in the lesson content or accepted disciplinary knowledge.
6. **Non-example or boundary case:** show something plausibly confused with the term and explain the decisive difference. Do not use an obviously unrelated item.
7. **Use in context:** include a short sentence, equation, caption, scenario, source excerpt, or worked step when context clarifies meaning.
8. **Student prompt:** ask learners to identify, explain, compare, classify, sketch, complete, or apply the concept.

Keep original source wording and detailed educator guidance in speaker notes when the visible explanation is simplified.

## Choose a Useful Representation

Select the representation that matches the content:

- **Frayer model:** definition, characteristics, examples, and non-examples for concepts with clear attributes and boundaries.
- **Concept map:** relationships among several terms, including labeled links when the relationship matters.
- **Compare and contrast:** two or three easily confused concepts with shared and distinguishing features.
- **Process or sequence:** ordered stages, cycles, procedures, or cause-and-effect chains.
- **Part-to-whole diagram:** systems, structures, hierarchies, and component relationships.
- **Morphology view:** meaningful prefix, root, and suffix analysis only when the word's construction reliably supports its current disciplinary meaning.
- **Multiple-meaning view:** everyday and subject-specific meanings shown side by side when students may confuse them.
- **Word wall view:** concise reference cards for repeated classroom use. Pair the reference with separate practice rather than treating display as instruction.

Do not use a Frayer model for every term by default. A representation is useful only when its structure matches the concept.

## Design Examples and Non-Examples

1. Make each example demonstrate the defining features of the concept.
2. Make each non-example plausible enough to reveal a misconception or boundary.
3. State why the example qualifies and why the non-example does not. Base the distinction on the definition, not appearance alone.
4. Use more than one example when the concept can appear in varied forms and students might overgeneralize from a single case.
5. Avoid examples that depend on cultural background, sensitive personal disclosure, stereotypes, or knowledge outside the supplied lesson.
6. Preserve valid ambiguity. Label a boundary case as `It depends` and state the deciding condition when classification is context-dependent.
7. Do not reveal the answer to an assessed problem or reproduce restricted source content as an example.

## Write Student-Facing Content

- Address students directly with concise actions such as `Notice...`, `Compare...`, `Explain...`, or `Try it...`.
- Use complete, concrete sentences and define unfamiliar language needed to understand the explanation.
- Preserve mathematical notation, scientific units, capitalization, pronunciation marks, and discipline-specific conventions.
- Add pronunciation only when supported by a reliable supplied source or standard reference. Never guess.
- Add translations or cognates only when requested or source-supported. Label false cognates and language variants accurately.
- Avoid circular definitions that define a term with the same term or an unexplained related word.
- Do not describe a student as a level or deficit. Explain what the concept shows and what the learner should notice or do.

## Practice for Understanding

Include practice that requires meaning, not transcription. Use a balanced subset of:

- Match a term to a new example, representation, or scenario
- Sort examples and non-examples, then justify one choice
- Complete a concept map or labeled relationship
- Compare two related terms using a decisive distinction
- Select the term that fits a context and explain why
- Correct a plausible misuse or misconception
- Draw, model, or annotate the concept
- Use the term accurately in a subject-specific explanation
- Retrieve a definition or feature before revealing choices

Move from recognition to recall or application when time allows. Do not ask students only to copy definitions, repeat every word in one sentence, or complete decorative word-search activities.

## Build the Slides

1. Preserve the current Slide Master, assigned layouts, theme colors, theme fonts, slide size, margins, and school or district branding.
2. Use native editable text, shapes, tables, connectors, and PowerPoint diagrams. Do not flatten instructional content into screenshots.
3. Give the term and core explanation the clearest hierarchy. Keep source notes, teacher prompts, and accepted answers in speaker notes.
4. Use one stable layout pattern across term slides while varying the representation when the concepts require it.
5. Keep diagrams and images large enough to inspect. Label essential parts directly and avoid decorative stock imagery.
6. Use connectors and spatial grouping only to express a real relationship. Label arrows with verbs or relationship phrases when direction is not self-evident.
7. Use short headings and readable body text. Split dense content across slides instead of shrinking type.
8. Pair color and icons with text labels. Never rely on color alone to mark examples, non-examples, categories, or correctness.
9. Add source attribution in speaker notes or visibly beside a quotation, excerpt, or licensed image when required.
10. Avoid gradients, 3D effects, dense decoration, and unrelated animation.

## Optional Reveal and Navigation

When the educator requests interactive practice:

1. Keep answers hidden when the practice slide opens.
2. Add a visible `Reveal response` control using a trigger with `Appear` or a short `Fade`.
3. When a trigger cannot be created reliably, use a linked answer slide with visible `Show response` and `Back to practice` controls.
4. For open-ended prompts, reveal criteria or one reasonable response rather than implying only one wording is correct.
5. Put detailed answer keys, accepted variants, likely misconceptions, and facilitation moves in speaker notes.
6. Test every trigger and hyperlink in Slide Show mode.
7. Do not add VBA, macros, add-ins, external quiz engines, or unsupported automatic scoring.

## Accessibility and Classroom Quality

- Keep text and labels readable from the intended viewing distance.
- Add descriptive alt text to every meaningful image, diagram, icon, and interactive control.
- Use a logical reading order: term, definition, explanation, representation, example, non-example, then prompt.
- Ensure diagrams remain understandable in grayscale and that labels do not depend on connector color alone.
- Use sufficient contrast and avoid placing text over visually busy imagery.
- Make reveal and navigation controls keyboard-orderable with generous click targets.
- Keep essential meaning available in edit view, printed handouts, and PDF export even when animation is unavailable.
- Avoid rapid motion, flashing, unnecessary sound, and animation that delays access to the content.

## Validation

Review the completed deck at fit-to-window and test interactive elements in Slide Show mode.

1. Confirm the selected terms are necessary for the stated objective and appropriate for the learner level and available time.
2. When using a Teach lesson plan, confirm every term and concept supports a stated Objective or required Lesson Outline activity.
3. Compare every definition, explanation, example, non-example, visual, symbol, formula, pronunciation, translation, and source claim with the supplied material or an identified reliable reference.
4. Confirm student-friendly explanations preserve every condition and distinction needed for accurate disciplinary meaning.
5. Confirm each visual teaches the concept and is accurately labeled rather than merely decorative.
6. Confirm each non-example is plausible and the deciding difference is explicit.
7. Confirm the deck shows meaningful relationships among concepts instead of presenting only disconnected cards.
8. Confirm practice includes retrieval or application and that answers are not visible in the opening state.
9. If reveals or linked answers are used, test every control and confirm open-ended prompts allow valid response variation.
10. Confirm no slide, text box, image, diagram, connector, label, or control is clipped, overlapped, distorted, or too small.
11. Confirm contrast, alt text, reading order, keyboard access, and print/PDF comprehension.
12. Report the source used, objective, learner level, selected terms, slide count, representation types, practice mode, answer mode, assumptions, unsupported content, and educator decisions still needed.
